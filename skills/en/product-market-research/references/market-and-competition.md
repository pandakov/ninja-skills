# Market, Competition, and Segment Selection

Use to assess market attractiveness, alternatives, positioning, and achievable scale.

## Market boundaries

Define the customer job, buyer unit, geography, organization or user types, eligible scenarios, currency, base year, and forecast horizon. State inclusions and exclusions. Distinguish potential users, paying customers, spending on the job, and supplier revenue.

Start with existing spending and solutions. The generative AI market rarely matches a specific product's market. Trends, investment growth, or search volume do not establish accessible paying demand.

Assess drivers and constraints: budgets, labor costs, workflow changes, regulation, model and data availability, and integration maturity. Verify dates and geographic applicability for changing conditions; distinguish forecasts from observations.

## Alternatives map

Include direct products, broader platforms with relevant features, internal development, services, manual work, and the status quo. Select alternatives by decision relevance, without a fixed company quota. An incomplete market scan cannot support a claim of no competition.

Compare for the same job and segment:

| Alternative | Audience and scenario | Outcome / workflow | Price and charging unit | Adoption, integrations, data | Constraints and switching | Sources and date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Include free tiers, trials, minimum contracts, limits, usage charges, and surcharges when material. “Contact sales” means unknown pricing. Missing feature information does not prove absence. Separate advertised capabilities and marketing promises from verified results.

Investigate selection and rejection reasons: trust, workflow fit, quality, purchasing ease, habits, and data control. A blank feature cell is not yet an opportunity. Explain who suffers from a proposed gap, how often, why they would pay to close it, and why competitors have not addressed it.

## Market sizing

Prefer bottom-up calculations, cross-checked with an independent top-down estimate where available. Avoid unsupported market-share assumptions.

- **TAM:** annual opportunity for the defined buyer category under the chosen charging model. For example, sum `paying entities per segment × realistic annual revenue per payer`.
- **SAM:** TAM accessible given geography, language, use case, integrations, data, rules, and service model. Handle overlapping filters jointly without excluding the same customers twice.
- **SOM:** SAM achievable by a stated deadline under specific sales, onboarding, and retention capacity. Ground it in channels and team constraints, not an arbitrary “capture 1%.”

For usage pricing: `eligible payers × paid operations per year × net revenue per operation`. For marketplaces, distinguish gross merchandise value (GMV) from platform revenue and apply the take rate explicitly. Use non-overlapping segments or correct for overlap. Do not multiply every employee by a company-wide license price.

Model SOM through a funnel by period: reachable suitable customers → contact → qualification → pilot → payment. Include sales delays, onboarding resources, repeat sales, and churn. Ending active customers equal starting customers plus acquisitions minus departures. Onboarding cannot exceed team capacity.

Distinguish actual first-year revenue from the annualized recurring revenue rate at year-end (ARR). Do not compare cumulative multi-year SOM revenue with annual TAM. For subscriptions acquired throughout the year, customer-months are more accurate than ending customers multiplied by the annual price.

For each input, show value or range, unit, period, and source or assumption. Build conservative, base, and optimistic scenarios when defensible. Vary meaningful drivers rather than applying the same percentage to every number. Test sensitivity to the most uncertain inputs. Without evidence, provide formulas, gaps, and a data collection plan instead of invented values.

## Segment priorities and positioning

Compare problem severity and frequency, current spending, buyer access, budget, data constraints, sales cycle, onboarding complexity, and economics. Use scores and weights only when helpful; explain them and avoid presenting subjective arithmetic as precise measurement.

Check hard constraints before ranking: no lawful access to required data, unacceptable error risk, or an infeasible budget or sales cycle may rule out a segment regardless of score. If small weight or assumption changes alter the winner, further validation is needed.

Frame positioning as: “For [segment in context], the product delivers [measurable outcome] through [mechanism], compared with [current alternative]; the reason to believe is [evidence or a hypothesis still to test].”

Distinguish an initial advantage from durable defensibility. For data, distribution, integrations, expertise, and accumulated quality, explain the mechanism and reproducibility conditions. Access to a popular model alone is not a durable advantage. Include incumbent platform entry in the recommendation when it could readily absorb the niche.
