# Evidence, Needs, and Problem Validation

Use for evidence collection, segmentation, workflow analysis, and interview preparation. Scale depth to the decision's importance.

## Search plan and evidence register

Frame research questions around decision uncertainties: who experiences the problem, how often, current solutions, who pays for what, and why existing options fall short. Select suitable evidence for each question and actively seek disconfirmation. Use the target market's language and terminology, including non-AI names for the job.

| Question | Useful evidence | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segment size and structure? | Official statistics, registries, industry research with methodology | Willingness to buy the product |
| What do alternatives offer? | Product pages, documentation, pricing, terms, demos | Actual effectiveness for every customer |
| How do pain and workflow manifest? | Interviews about specific events, observation, support requests, reviews | Population-wide prevalence |
| Demand and willingness to pay? | Purchase history, paid pilots, paid repeat usage, reasons for refusal | Scalable demand without channel and retention testing |
| Adoption barriers? | Procurement requirements, IT/security interviews, contracts | The same constraint applying to every company |

Maintain a compact register for material claims:

| ID | Claim and status | Source / document page | Publication and access dates | Period, geography, sample | Reliability and limitation | Decision impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

For calculations, provide formulas and source IDs for inputs; for hypotheses, the rationale and test; for gaps, the missing evidence. Avoid false precision through confidence percentages. Use explained confidence levels:

- High: direct, current evidence for the relevant context, with independent verification where needed.
- Medium: relevant indirect or limited evidence with explicit material assumptions.
- Low: analogy, biased samples, unverified claims, or substantial context mismatch.

Official pricing supports the advertised price on the access date, not actual average customer spending. Multiple articles repeating one report count as one source. A vendor case study supports a claimed outcome in that case, not an independent estimate of typical impact.

When estimates conflict, first compare definitions, years, geography, currencies, and methods. Do not average incompatible values. Preserve unresolved ranges and show whether the decision changes at their boundaries. Disclose inaccessible pages; do not cite them as fully read.

Stop expanding the search when key questions have enough evidence for the decision, new sources no longer change conclusions, and remaining unknowns are better tested experimentally. If time or access limits stop research, list untested hypotheses.

## Segmentation and customer job

Define segments by work context: trigger, task volume, current alternative, cost of errors, budget, data access, constraints, and buying process. Identify:

- Users and beneficiaries.
- Buyers and budget owners.
- Influencers and adoption blockers.

These roles may coincide in B2C; avoid artificial complexity. Frame the job as: “When [situation], I need [outcome], so that [benefit].” Treat it as a hypothesis until supported.

Reconstruct the current workflow step by step. Assess frequency, active time, waiting, errors, rework, and tool switching. Distinguish cash savings from freed capacity. Do not monetize every saved minute without explaining how it becomes an economic benefit.

## Problem interviews

When preparing research, recruit across current alternatives, problem intensity, and buying roles. Include rejecters, former customers, and people who consider the problem unimportant. Friends and followers are a convenience sample, not a representative one. Justify qualitative sample size through segment diversity and recurring themes, not statistical significance.

Useful questions:

1. Recall the last time you handled this task. What triggered it?
2. Show or describe the steps, tools, and participants. Where did delays and errors occur?
3. What were the consequences? Which measurements or records support that?
4. What have you tried to change? Why choose the current approach, and what works well?
5. When did you last pay for a similar solution? Which budget funded it, and who approved it?
6. What would trigger a search for a replacement? What would prevent switching?
7. What data and workflow access could a pilot receive? Who decides?

Do not open problem interviews by pitching the AI solution. “Would you use it?” is weak evidence. Test the concept separately and record concrete commitments: workflow access, buyer participation, an agreed pilot, or payment. Hypothetical prices do not establish willingness to pay.

## Synthesis

Group observations by segment and context. Preserve contrary examples, refusal reasons, and adequate existing alternatives. When reporting mention frequency, include numerator, denominator, and recruitment method; do not extrapolate a small qualitative sample to the market.

Deliver prioritized problems, triggering situations, current costs, buying roles, confidence per conclusion, and next tests. Quote only actual materials with appropriate attribution; avoid unnecessary personal data.
